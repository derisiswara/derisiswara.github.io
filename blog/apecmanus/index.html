Slide 1

Good afternoon, everyone…
My name is Deri Siswara from IPB University, Indonesia.
Thank you for having me here; I’m very grateful for this opportunity.
I’m here to present our study or research on the implementation of food safety in the APEC region. Specifically, we conducted a simple survey to evaluate how food-safety practices are applied in MSMEs and street-food businesses across the region.

Let’s begin with Why does food safety matter for MSMEs and street-food businesses in the APEC region?

Slide 2

98% of APEC businesses are MSMEs, and about half operate in the food sector.

Then, MSMEs and street-food businesses are a major part of this region’s economy as local and international culinary-tourism. Our food businesses serve as cultural ambassadors, showcasing unique local varieties and traditions that attract tourists.

Lastly, quality and cleanliness concerns: many food MSMEs face challenges—in maintaining proper hygiene standards and food-safety protocols—due to resource constraints.

So, in general, food safety is very important to ensure public health and consumer trust.

This study evaluates food-safety implementation among MSMEs and street-food businesses in the APEC region, with one big question: could these businesses comply with international food-safety standards like ISO 22000, HACCP, and GMP?

Slide 3

We conducted a simple survey based on criteria from the ISO Food Safety Management System, HACCP, and GMP. The elements from these standards were included in our questionnaire.

Our repodents are food business actors and government regulators. In total, we surveyed 59 participants across the APEC region:

  • 50 MSME and street-food business actors

  • 9 government regulators

Note: This was a simple survey. Non-probability survey using a convenience sampling method—we interviewed whoever was immediately available. While this design cannot represent the entire population, it still provides valuable insights.

Slide 4

Let’s recap ISO 22000:2018 Food Safety Management System, the international standard for our study. Just a short summary—not the full detail, since I’m not an expert.

This standard establishes a systematic approach to food safety through the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, fully incorporating HACCP principles and integrating Good Manufacturing Practices within its framework.

It gives us an ideal structure to evaluate existing practices and implement improvements. ISO 22000 embeds two nested PDCA loops. Overall FSMS (Clauses 4-7, 9-10) and one inside Clause 8 (Operating) for hazard control.

Notes: Hazard control is any action you take to prevent, eliminate, or reduce food-safety risks (biological, chemical, or physical) to a level that won’t harm consumers.

Slides 5: Fidings

“Here, we’re looking at Clauses 4, 5, and 6 of ISO 22000—the core of the Plan phase in the PDCA cycle.

First, notice the three colored semicircles:

  • Context (red): Q1–Q4 and Q82 score mostly between 60 and 90 percent. That means vendors generally understand their business setting and legal basics.
  • Planning (blue): Q5–Q12 score 76 to 100 percent. Vendors know who’s responsible, what procedures to follow, and how to track feedback and evaluations.
  • Leadership (green): Q3–Q6 score 84 to 98 percent, showing clear assignment of food-safety roles.

But look at Q4—the red bar for “Product distribution permit.” Less than half of vendors hold this permit. Without it, there’s no formal check or traceability for their products.

Key takeaway: Vendors plan well, but they need help closing the permit gap. Simplifying permit rules or offering provisional approvals could bring more vendors into full compliance.”

Slide 6

On this slide, you see a word cloud of the many types of food vendors we surveyed—everything from satay and gudeg stalls to smoothie carts and home-kitchen operators.

Most of these small vendors are exempt from a formal product-distribution permit. Regulators usually waive the permit when:

  • The food is ready-to-eat and has a shelf life under seven days,
  • It’s packaged right in front of the customer,
  • The operation is very small—think a single cart or a family kitchen—
  • And only basic processing steps (washing, cutting, mixing) are used.

Because of these exemptions—and the time, cost, and paperwork involved—many vendors simply never apply for the permit.

But exemption doesn’t remove their responsibility to keep food safe. By following simple ISO 22000 steps—good hygiene, quick hazard checks, short daily records, basic staff training, and fast corrective action—these vendors can protect customers without the burden of full certification.

Slide 7

Now let’s look at Clause 7, still part of the Plan phase in ISO 22000. This covers the resources, work environment, product controls, employee competence, and documentation.

As you can see:

  • Infrastructure scores about 80 percent, and Work Environment about 79 percent—so most vendors have basic facilities and workspace set up.
  • Product & Quality Control is 75 percent, and Employee Competence is 73 percent—meaning training and quality checks happen regularly.

But look at Documented Information—only 32 percent compliance. That means very few keep written records of procedures, logs, or training certificates.

Key point: Vendors generally have the right tools, people, and processes—but they rarely write it down. Without records, it’s hard to prove you followed safety steps or to track issues over time.

So our recommendation here is simple: introduce short, easy forms or checklists that take just a minute to fill out. That small step will close this documentation gap and strengthen the whole system.

Slide 8

On this slide, we dive deeper into Clause 7, which covers human resources, infrastructure, and documented information.

First, look at the blue and red bars under Human Resources:

  • Only around 50 percent of vendors confirm regular health checks (Q19) and special attire (Q17), and fewer than half prohibit jewelry or separate cashier duties (Q21, Q23).
  • This means many vendors lack basic worker‐health monitoring and hygiene training.

Next, under Infrastructure:

  • About 50 percent report food pests on site (Q28) and personal items in processing areas (Q29).

Finally, Documented Information is the weakest link:

  • Only 36 percent keep raw-material or operations records (Q73), and just 26 percent record worker health or training (Q74).

Bottom line: Vendors have some facilities and know the rules—but they often skip health checks, pest control, and, worst of all, don’t write it down. Without records, it’s impossible to prove safe practices or learn from mistakes.

Recommendation: Introduce very short checklists or mobile-friendly log apps for health checks, pest sightings, and daily process notes. These tools take under a minute a day but will dramatically improve compliance and traceability.

Slide 9

Here we’re on Clause 8, the heart of the FSMS where most HACCP steps happen in daily operations.

Look at the bars:

  • Machine & equipment storage (Q79) hits 100 percent, and production oversight (Q81) is 96 percent—so almost everyone keeps tools clean and assigns a responsible person.
  • Dry-place storage (Q77) and hazard separation (Q78) score 98 and 90 percent—solid performance there.
  • FIFO labeling (Q76) and materials storage (Q75) are 82 and 96 percent, showing most vendors mark dates and protect ingredients correctly.

But notice Q80—“Food packaging clearly labeled”—only 26 percent compliance. This gap tells us that many ready-to-eat vendors skip proper labels, making it hard for consumers to know when to eat or throw out a product.

Key takeaway: Daily operations are well handled, but labeling must improve. A simple, low-cost fix is to provide pre-printed date-sticker sheets or colored labels. That small step will help customers use products safely and complete the food-safety cycle.

Slide 10

Finally, Clauses 9 and 10 cover how vendors monitor their processes and improve over time. We looked at three things: periodic evaluations, customer feedback, and complaints.

  • On the left, you see that about 24 percent of vendors do not run any periodic self-checks.
  • In the middle, you’ll notice a significant link between periodic evaluations and asking for customer feedback (p = 0.011). Vendors who skip their own reviews almost never seek feedback.
  • On the right, there’s also a strong link between evaluations and customer complaints (p = 0.017). If you don’t check your process, you’re less likely to hear—and learn from—any complaints.

Why this matters: Without regular reviews and customer input, a vendor’s routine stays the same every day—good or bad—because there’s no trigger to fix problems or try new ideas.

Quick fix: Encourage a five-minute monthly review, paired with a simple feedback box or quick survey. That small habit will help vendors catch issues early and keep improving food safety.

Slide 11

Here we switch to the regulators’ view on legality and permit systems, covering Clauses 4.2 and 4.3. Each horizontal bar shows the percentage of regulators who answered “yes” to key questions:

  • Q5 (100%): Every economy has systems or procedures in place to grant permits to MSMEs and street-food vendors.
  • Q4 (89%) and Q6 (88%): Most offer a permit-registration service and a complaint service for those permits.
  • Q1 (89%): Nearly all have local regulations specifically for MSME or street-food supervision.
  • Q3 (78%): A smaller share requires explicit registration or permits for these small vendors.
  • Q8 (50%): Crucially, only half of the economies charge a fee for a business license; the other half provide it free of charge.

Key message: Regulators have built the legal framework and support services—yet the fee structure is split evenly across the region. Making licensing free or subsidized could remove a major barrier for small vendors, helping more of them meet food-safety requirements.

Slide 12

On this final slide, we look at regulator supervision and its impact.

  • 83 percent of economies have formal supervision plans and conduct at least annual inspections to check food‐safety compliance.
  • However, only 33 percent actually publish their inspection results. That lack of transparency means vendors—and the public—can’t see which sites passed or failed.
  • Despite inspections, 50 percent of regulators report ongoing food-poisoning cases in the street-food sector.

Below, the word cloud highlights the top concern: cleanliness. Regulators tell us that, even with rules and inspections, many vendors still struggle with basic hygiene and waste management.

Key takeaway: Regulators are active, but publishing results and sharing data could motivate vendors to improve. And targeting the root issue—cleanliness—through public cafés, mobile training units, or “Clean Street-Food” awards will help reduce poisonings and raise standards across the region.

Slide 13

Finally, we look at how regulators foster food safety through training and improvement programs, tied to Clause 7 of ISO 22000 (support resources).

  • 100 percent of regulators report they provide general training to MSMEs and street-food vendors (Q18).
  • However, only 71 percent offer specific food-safety training (Q19), and the same share run dedicated improvement programs (Q22).

This tells us: regulators are committed to capacity building, but nearly 30 percent lack targeted food-safety workshops or hands-on coaching.

Why it matters: General business training is helpful, but without tailored sessions on hazard control, hygiene checks, or record-keeping, vendors miss out on critical skills.

Quick recommendation: Expand and standardize modular food-safety courses—for example, a one-day HACCP basics bootcamp or quick online modules—so every vendor can access the specific know-how they need to keep food safe.

Slide 14

Let me wrap up with our four main takeaways and quick recommendations:

  1. Planning gaps (Clauses 4–7): Most vendors meet basic planning requirements, but they often skip distribution permits, health checks, and written records. These are commonly exempt, but exemptions don’t remove the responsibility to keep food safe.

  2. Operational gaps (Clause 8): Daily operations run smoothly, yet only 26 percent of vendors clearly label their packaging—making it hard for consumers to know expiry dates. A simple date-sticker can fix this.

  3. Improvement gaps (Clauses 9–10): About 24 percent of businesses never do self-checks or ask for feedback, so they miss chances to learn and improve. We recommend a five-minute monthly review plus a quick feedback box.

  4. Regulator support: Regulators have laws and inspections in place, but only half charge fees, and only 71 percent offer specific food-safety training. To help vendors, regulators should simplify licensing, publish inspection results, and expand modular food-safety courses.

By closing these small but critical gaps—with permits, labels, records, and training—we can make sure even the tiniest street stall serves safe, trusted food across APEC.
